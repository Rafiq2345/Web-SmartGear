Sony WH-1000XM6 Review (2026) — The King of Active Noise Cancellation?

Sony's latest flagship over-ear ANC headphones introduce QN3 noise cancellation with a 12-microphone array, 30mm carbon composite drivers, LDAC high-res audio, and a folding design with all-day headband ergonomics.

1. Active Noise Cancellation & Attenuation

The Sony WH-1000XM6 features an upgraded 12-microphone array controlled by Sony's HD Noise Cancelling Processor QN3, which Sony says is seven times faster than the QN1 chip in the WH-1000XM5. That speed matters because the processor re-tunes the cancellation in real time: Sony's Adaptive NC Optimizer continuously adjusts for outside noise, air pressure, and even how the headphones sit on your head — including while wearing a hat or glasses — so the quiet holds up when a plane climbs or a train pulls out of a station. The result is strong attenuation across low-frequency airplane rumble and train noise as well as mid-frequency cafe chatter, the range where cheaper ANC headphones usually give up.

Independent lab testing backs up the flagship claim, with an important qualification: the XM6 wins on breadth, not by a landslide. RTINGS calls the WH-1000XM6 the best noise-cancelling headphones it has tested, scoring its common-scenario noise isolation at 9.9, ahead of the Bose QuietComfort Ultra at 9.5 and the WH-1000XM5 at 9.4. SoundGuys' lab measurements tell a similar story, with the XM6 reducing average loudness by 87% against 85% for the Bose QuietComfort Ultra, and an ANC score of 8.7 out of 10. Where the Sony pulls furthest ahead is the mid-range — voices, office chatter, keyboard clatter and street traffic — which is exactly the noise that matters most to office workers and commuters, since low rumble is the easy part for any good ANC headphone.

There are two honest limits to know about. First, Sony does not let you set the cancellation strength yourself: unlike Bose, which offers adjustable ANC presets in its app, the XM6 gives you noise cancelling on, ambient sound modes, or off, and trusts the Adaptive NC Optimizer to do the rest. Most buyers will never miss the manual control, but if you like fine-tuning the level for a quiet library versus a loud platform, Bose is more flexible. Second, the cancellation depends on a good seal, and the XM6's earcups are shallower than some rivals'. RTINGS notes that the pads do not always form the best seal, especially if you wear glasses, and a broken seal costs you bass and low-frequency cancellation first. Try them with your own glasses on before deciding, and use the app's fit check if the seal feels uneven.

2. Acoustic Quality & LDAC High-Resolution Codecs

Equipped with a lightweight 30mm carbon fiber dome with a soft polyurethane edge, the WH-1000XM6 improves high-frequency clarity while controlling sub-bass bleed. Support for Sony's proprietary LDAC codec enables wireless bitrates up to 990 kbps at 24-bit/96kHz when connected to compatible Android devices.

Out of the box the XM6 sounds warm and bass-forward rather than neutral. RTINGS measures bass emphasized by about 5 dB with treble sitting around 1 dB below its target, and describes the overall signature as warm — punchy and easy to listen to for hours, but not a reference tuning. SoundGuys' panel-based MDAQS audio-quality model scores the XM6 at 4.8 out of 5, putting it in the top 10% of the over-ear headphones it has measured, and What Hi-Fi? awarded the XM6 five stars, calling it the most detailed, dynamic, precise and open sound it had heard from a wireless Sony flagship. The tuning was co-created with mastering engineers, and that shows in vocal clarity and instrument separation; the trade-off is that bass-heavy tracks can sound thick, and some listeners will want to trim the low end.

The fix for that is built in: Sony's Sound Connect app offers a 10-band equalizer with presets, so the bass shelf that bothers critical listeners is adjustable in seconds rather than a reason to return the headphones. The app is also where the rest of the sound features live — DSEE Extreme upscaling for compressed streaming audio, 360 Reality Audio spatial sound with head tracking on compatible Android phones, and Adaptive Sound Control that changes settings by location. Codec support is the broadest in this price class on Android: LDAC, AAC, SBC and LC3 over Bluetooth 5.3, with LE Audio and Auracast broadcast audio. One genuine omission is wired digital audio: the USB-C port charges only and carries no audio, so wired listening means the included 3.5mm analogue cable, unlike Bose's second-generation QuietComfort Ultra, which supports lossless audio over USB-C.

Who actually hears the LDAC advantage is worth spelling out, because it decides whether the XM6 is worth its premium over cheaper rivals. Android listeners with a compatible phone and high-quality streaming files get the full 990 kbps pipeline; iPhone listeners get AAC only, since Apple does not support LDAC, and gain nothing from Sony's headline codec. In a quiet room with good recordings the difference between LDAC and AAC is audible as slightly cleaner highs and a more open soundstage; on a noisy train at moderate volume, it mostly is not. If you listen mainly on an iPhone, judge the XM6 on its noise cancelling, battery and comfort instead — the codec is not a reason to buy it.

3. Redesigned Comfort & Clamping Force

Weighing 254 grams, the WH-1000XM6 features soft-fit synthetic leather earpads and a widened headband cushion designed to spread pressure more evenly across the head, a design aimed at long listening sessions. The wider headband is a deliberate change from the XM5: it spreads the weight across more of the crown instead of concentrating it in one strip, which is the difference between headphones you forget after an hour and headphones you keep adjusting. The earpads are also user-replaceable by hand, without tools, which matters for a flagship you may keep for years — pads are the first part to wear out, and replacing them is cheaper than replacing headphones.

The other comfort story is portability. The XM6 brings back the folding design the XM5 dropped: the earcups fold flat and swivel into a redesigned magnetic book-style case that is noticeably easier to pack than the XM5's case, and it is the single most practical upgrade for travellers. The headphones come in Black, Midnight Blue, Platinum Silver, Sand Pink and Sandstone, and the folding hinge is part of why the XM6 feels like a travel product again rather than a desk product.

The caveats are real but narrow. SoundGuys found the earcup cavities relatively shallow, so the inner driver mesh can press against larger ears during long sessions, and RTINGS flags the same shallow pads as the reason the seal — and therefore the bass — can vary from wearer to wearer, particularly with glasses. In RTINGS' three-way comparison the Bose QuietComfort Ultra was the most comfortable of the group with the Sony as runner-up, which matches the general pattern: Bose is the pillow, Sony is the lighter, slightly firmer fit. There is also no ingress protection rating — Sony's own documentation stresses the headphones are susceptible to moisture — so these are not gym or rain headphones, and buyers who work out in over-ears should look elsewhere.

4. Battery Life & Beamforming Microphone Calls

With up to 30 hours of continuous playback with ANC enabled, the WH-1000XM6 easily lasts through a full work week of commuting. Sony rates music playback at up to 30 hours with noise cancelling on and 40 hours with it off, and calls at up to 24 hours with noise cancelling on and 28 hours off — so even a call-heavy workday barely dents a charge. The headline number is the same as the XM5's, which is why some buyers assume the battery did not improve, but the independent measurements tell a more interesting story: SoundGuys recorded 37 hours and 14 minutes of continuous playback with ANC on at a standard 75 dB output, comfortably beating Sony's 30-hour claim and the XM5's measured 31 hours and 53 minutes, while RTINGS measured about 31.75 hours in its own continuous test. The gap between the two labs is normal — volume, codec, ANC load and test method all move the result — but both labs beat Sony's rating, which is the opposite of the usual manufacturer optimism.

Charging is fast where it counts. A 3-minute charge over USB-PD gives about 3 hours of playback, which turns a dead battery at the airport into a usable pair before boarding finishes — though note the USB-PD adapter and the USB-C cable are not included in the box. The headphones can also keep playing while charging, so a power bank and a cable rescue a long-haul flight even if you forgot to charge overnight. Realistically, most owners will charge once or twice a week: a 30-plus-hour rating means a Monday charge covers commuting, office use and evening listening until Thursday or Friday.

Call quality is the XM6's quietest upgrade and one of its strongest. Six beamforming microphones with AI-trained noise reduction isolate your voice from background noise, and RTINGS named the XM6 the best microphone of the three flagships in its comparison against the XM5 and the Bose QuietComfort Ultra, noting that it isolates speech from coffee-shop noise and wind without dropping syllables. That matters more than it sounds: a headphone that is great for music but muffled on calls forces you to own a second headset for work, while the XM6 can credibly be the only headset on your desk. There is a dedicated microphone mute control, and multipoint pairing means a call on your phone pauses music from your laptop automatically.

5. App, Codecs & Everyday Features

The Sony Sound Connect app is the deepest companion app in this price class, and it is where much of the XM6's value hides. Beyond the 10-band equalizer, it offers Adaptive Sound Control, which learns the places you visit and adjusts noise cancelling and ambient sound automatically; Speak-to-Chat, which pauses music when you start talking to someone; Quick Access to Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music and Endel; and voice-assistant support for Google Assistant, Alexa and Gemini on compatible Android phones. Touch controls on the right earcup handle volume, track skipping and calls, while a redesigned physical power and Bluetooth button is easier to find by feel than the XM5's. Multipoint pairing connects two devices at once — laptop and phone is the classic pair — and switching is automatic when a call comes in.

Two features deserve a second look before you buy. First, Auracast broadcast audio and LE Audio arrive via the app and firmware, which is good for future-proofing but means the out-of-the-box experience depends on updates being installed — check for a firmware update on day one rather than judging the feature list on the box. Second, the sheer depth of the app can be a drawback: there are enough toggles, modes and location rules to spend an evening configuring, and buyers who want headphones that simply work may prefer Bose's simpler app. Our view is that the depth is a net win, because the two settings most people actually change — the equalizer and the ambient-sound level — are on the app's front screen, and everything else can be ignored.

6. Real-World Use: Flights, Office, Commuting & Calls

On flights, the XM6 is in its element. The Adaptive NC Optimizer adjusts for cabin air pressure as the plane climbs, the folding design and magnetic case make the headphones easy to stow, and the battery — 30 hours rated, over 31 hours in RTINGS' test and over 37 hours in SoundGuys' test — covers even a return long-haul trip without a recharge. The 3-minute quick charge is genuinely useful at the gate, and the 3.5mm cable covers the in-flight entertainment system, where Bluetooth is usually not an option. If you fly monthly or more, this is the use case that justifies the flagship price on its own.

In an open-plan office, the XM6's strength is the mid-range: voices, keyboard clatter and air-conditioning hum are the noises that break concentration, and independent testing shows the XM6 handles that band better than its predecessor and its Bose rival. Multipoint pairing keeps laptop music and phone calls on the same headset, Speak-to-Chat lets you answer a colleague without pulling the headphones off, and the ambient-sound mode can be set to let voices through while keeping the low drone out. For commuting, the same logic applies on trains and buses, with one addition: wind noise is handled well by the microphone array, and the quick charge rescues the mornings you forget to plug in overnight.

For calls, the XM6 can replace a dedicated office headset — the six-microphone AI beamforming system was the best microphone in RTINGS' flagship comparison — and for home listening the LDAC pipeline rewards good recordings on Android. The one environment where we would not recommend the XM6 is the gym: there is no water or sweat resistance rating, the shallow earcups can shift with intense movement, and RTINGS flags the fit as unstable during vigorous exercise. For workouts, a cheap pair of sport earbuds is a better tool than a $450 flagship.

7. Sony WH-1000XM6 vs Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen)

The Bose QuietComfort Ultra Headphones (2nd Gen) are the XM6's only true rival, and the contest is closer than the spec sheets suggest. Bose announced the second generation on 10 September 2025 and released it on 2 October 2025 at $449 — the same launch price as the Sony — with a 30-hour battery rating, Bluetooth 5.4, aptX Adaptive, and Immersive Audio spatial sound with head tracking, including a Cinema mode. Bose also fixed the first generation's two biggest weaknesses: battery life rose from 24 to 30 hours, and the USB-C port now supports lossless wired audio, which Sony still does not offer. Bose rates the second generation at up to 30 hours with noise cancelling on, 45 hours with it off, and 23 hours with Immersive Audio running.

Measured side by side, the Sony wins on endurance and cancellation, the Bose wins on comfort and flexibility. SoundGuys' standardized battery test ran the Sony for 37 hours and 14 minutes with ANC on against about 27 hours for the Bose — a ten-hour gap that no spec sheet reveals, since both are rated at 30 hours. RTINGS scores the Sony's noise isolation at 9.9 against the Bose's 9.5, and names the Sony the best microphone of the group, while naming the Bose the most comfortable. Bose counters with adjustable ANC presets in its app, a feature Sony does not offer; lossless audio over USB-C; and a slightly more forgiving fit for glasses wearers. The Bose is also the better pick for iPhone owners who want spatial audio, since Sony's LDAC advantage does not apply to them and Bose's Immersive Audio works regardless of phone.

Our read of the comparison is that most buyers should choose on ecosystem and comfort, not on a single test score. Android listeners, frequent flyers and call-heavy remote workers get more from the Sony: better measured battery, better measured cancellation, better calls, and LDAC. iPhone listeners who prioritize comfort, spatial audio and wired lossless playback get more from the Bose. The gap between them is small enough that trying both on for ten minutes — with your own glasses, if you wear them — is worth more than any review score, including ours.

8. XM6 vs XM5: Should You Upgrade?

The WH-1000XM5 launched in 2022 at $399.99 and remains on sale, often discounted, which makes the upgrade question a real one. Against the XM5, the XM6 brings the QN3 processor (Sony says seven times faster than the XM5's QN1), a 12-microphone array against the XM5's eight, the return of the folding design, LC3 and LE Audio support, a wider headband, and better call quality from its six-microphone beamforming system. Battery ratings are identical at 30 hours with ANC on, but SoundGuys measured the XM6 at 37 hours and 14 minutes against the XM5's 31 hours and 53 minutes, so the real-world endurance gap is about five hours. Sound quality improves too, with smoother bass and less fatiguing treble in SoundGuys' testing, though RTINGS found only a subtle mid-range gain.

Our advice is straightforward. If you are buying your first flagship pair, buy the XM6: the folding design, the stronger ANC and the better calls are worth the premium over a discounted XM5 for most buyers. If you already own the XM5, upgrade only if the missing fold annoys you every trip, you take a lot of calls, or you want the strongest ANC available — the sound and battery improvements alone are not transformative. And if your budget is tight, a discounted XM5 remains a genuinely good buy: it keeps most of the XM6's sound and the same rated battery life for substantially less money.

9. Price & Value: What $449.99 Buys in November 2026

The XM6 launched on 15 May 2025 at $449.99 in the US — $50 more than the XM5's launch price — and that launch price is the number most reviews still quote. Sony's own US store now lists the headphones at $459.99, with the Black model on sale at $379.99 at the time of our check, and Amazon prices were reported around $378 in early October 2026. Prices were checked on 17 November 2026; street prices move with sales events, so check the live price before buying rather than trusting any single figure in this review. At full price the XM6 sits in the same tier as the Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) at $449, and well below the AirPods Max 2 at $549.

Is it good value? At the $379–$399 street prices seen during sale periods, yes without hesitation — that is flagship ANC, class-leading measured battery and the best call quality in the class for roughly the price the XM5 launched at. At the $459.99 list price, the case is narrower: you are paying for the best measured noise cancellation and the deepest feature set, and buyers who mainly listen at home in quiet rooms, or on an iPhone without LDAC, will get most of the experience from a cheaper pair. The XM6's value also depends on how long you keep it — user-replaceable earpads, a folding hinge built to answer the XM5's breakage complaints, and firmware-updated features like Auracast all point to a headphone designed to last several years rather than one upgrade cycle.

10. What We'd Buy and Why

If we were spending our own money on one pair of flagship ANC headphones, we would buy the Sony WH-1000XM6 — on sale, ideally at or below $400 — for the combination no rival matches: the best measured noise cancellation in RTINGS' testing, the longest measured battery in SoundGuys' testing, the best call quality of the three flagships RTINGS compared, and a folding design that actually travels. The deciding factors are practical rather than audiophile: the XM6 is the pair that disappears on a long flight, survives a week of commuting on one charge, and handles a work call from a noisy cafe without a second headset.

We would buy the Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) instead if we lived on an iPhone, valued comfort above all else, or wanted lossless audio over USB-C and adjustable ANC strength — Bose wins all three of those contests, and the two headphones cost the same. We would buy a discounted WH-1000XM5 if the budget was the deciding factor and the missing fold did not matter. And we would buy neither if the main use were the gym or running in the rain, since neither flagship carries a water-resistance rating. For the buyer this review is written for — a traveller, commuter or remote worker who wants one pair that does everything — the XM6 is our pick, which is why it keeps its 9.4 out of 10.

12. Final Verdict

The WH-1000XM6 is the noise-cancelling headphone to beat: QN3 processing with a 12-microphone array delivers class-leading cancellation across all frequencies, the 30-hour rated battery — over 37 hours in SoundGuys' lab test — with 3-minute emergency charging removes battery anxiety entirely, and the 254g frame with memory foam cushions stays comfortable for long sessions. The $449.99 launch price (now listed at $459.99 by Sony, frequently discounted to around $379–$399) is premium, and LDAC's full 990kbps needs a strong Bluetooth signal — but for travelers and Android listeners who want the best ANC available, nothing else in this review's comparison comes close. Buy it on sale if you can; at full price it still earns its keep if you fly, commute, or take calls in noisy places every week. If you listen mostly on an iPhone in quiet rooms, the Bose QuietComfort Ultra (2nd Gen) or a discounted XM5 will serve you as well for less.

13. Frequently Asked Questions

Is this really the best noise cancelling available?

Yes, on the published evidence: the QN3 processor with a 12-microphone array is Sony's flagship ANC setup, and independent lab testing backs it up — RTINGS rates it the best noise-cancelling headphones it has tested, and SoundGuys measured 87% average loudness reduction. That breadth is what separates flagship ANC from mid-range ANC — voices, office chatter, and engine drone all drop, not just airplane hum. We did not lab-test a unit ourselves; the measurements quoted are the labs', not ours.

Is LDAC worth it, and who benefits?

Android listeners, primarily: LDAC streams at up to 990kbps, far above AAC and SBC, and this review's codec section covers the difference in detail. The caveat is in the cons — LDAC needs a high Bluetooth signal strength to hold 990kbps without drops, so crowded wireless environments can force it to a lower bitrate. iPhone listeners, who get AAC only, gain nothing from LDAC either way.

How good is the battery life in practice?

Thirty hours with ANC on and 40 hours with it off, against a 20–24-hour industry standard — roughly a full work week of commuting and office use per charge. And the emergency top-up is genuinely useful: 3 minutes on USB-PD gives 3 hours of playback, against the competitors' 10 minutes for 2 hours.

Are they comfortable for all-day wear?

Yes: at 254g with a foldable swivel design and plush memory foam ear cushions, they sit at the light end of the flagship range (competitors run 250–385g), and this review's comfort section covers clamping force in detail. The swivel fold also makes them the easiest flagship to pack for travel.

Are they worth $459.99?

If noise cancelling and battery are your priorities, yes — no competitor in this review's table matches both the QN3 ANC and the 30-hour battery. If you mainly listen at home in quiet rooms, or you are on iPhone without LDAC, a cheaper pair gives you most of the sound for less money, and the premium buys features you will not use.

Who should buy them, in short: travelers, commuters, and Android listeners who want the strongest noise cancelling available with a 30-hour battery and LDAC high-resolution audio — this is the flagship ANC headphone, and its QN3 processing justifies the price for those users. Who should skip them: iPhone listeners, who gain nothing from LDAC and can find equal everyday performance for less, and quiet-room listeners, whose environment never tests the ANC that makes these headphones special.
