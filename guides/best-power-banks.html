Key Buying Factors for Portable Power Banks

Choosing the right power bank requires matching its USB Power Delivery (USB-PD) output wattage and battery capacity to your daily mobile devices.

1. USB-PD Wattage & Fast-Charging Standards

  • Smartphones (20W – 60W): Modern iPhones now peak well above the old 27W ceiling — the iPhone 17 Pro peaks up to 40W (Apple specifies a 40W+ adapter for ~50% in 20 minutes; ChargerLAB measured peaks of 36–39W), the iPhone 16 Pro Max peaked around 39W in lab testing (an instantaneous peak; sustained charging stayed near 30W), and the iPhone 18 Pro goes up to 60W. 27W was the iPhone 15-era ceiling. Samsung Galaxy flagship devices require Programmable Power Supply (PPS) protocol support to achieve full 45W Super Fast Charging 2.0.
  • Laptops & Tablets (65W – 100W): Ultraportable laptops like the MacBook Air M5 require a minimum of 45W to 65W to charge efficiently during active use. High-performance 15-inch and 16-inch laptops benefit from 100W single-port delivery.
  • Multi-Port Power Allocation: When charging multiple devices simultaneously, power banks split their total wattage across active ports. High-tier GaN chargers maintain dedicated high wattage on primary ports to prevent laptop throttling.

2. FAA & TSA Airline Travel Regulations (100Wh Limit)

Aviation security guidelines (including TSA and FAA regulations) restrict carry-on lithium-ion batteries to a maximum of 100 Watt-hours (Wh) per battery without requiring special airline pre-approval. Power banks rated at 20,000mAh to 26,800mAh (at nominal 3.6V–3.7V cell voltage) typically deliver 72Wh to 99Wh, making them universally approved for carry-on luggage.

3. Form Factor & Daily Portability

  • Pocket-Sized (5,000 – 10,000mAh): Compact designs with integrated cables that fit into pockets for emergency smartphone recharges throughout campus or city commutes.
  • Ultra-Slim Flat Packs: Flat rectangular chargers (under 0.8 inches) that slide flat into laptop backpack sleeves alongside tablets and notebooks.
  • High-Capacity Power Stations (20,000mAh+): Dense battery packs designed for multi-day travel, off-grid work, and remote laptop powering.

The Prime in real use

The Prime is the bank for the two-laptop traveler: a consultant with a MacBook Pro and a Windows laptop, or a creator with a laptop and a tablet, both charging at full speed from one brick. The smart display is the feature that justifies the size — it shows live watts per port and time-to-full, so you know before a flight whether the bank will be ready, instead of guessing from four blinking dots. Two things to know before you buy. First, 100W from a USB-C port needs a 5A E-marked USB-C cable; a standard 60W cable caps the same port at 60W, and the cable in the box is the one to keep. Second, the 75-minute self-recharge needs a 100W wall charger, which is not included — with a 30W phone charger the same refill takes most of an afternoon. At about 545g it is also the heaviest bank in this guide, a real trade-off against the Blade below. One safety note worth the sentence: Anker has recalled several power-bank models in 2025–2026, so check any bank against Anker's recall page before buying — the A1336 Prime was not among the recalled models in a July 2026 check, but recalls are updated, and the check takes a minute.

The Blade in real use

The Blade's trick is its shape, not its spec sheet. At 0.7 inches thick and 445g it slides flat into a laptop sleeve next to the laptop itself, where a brick-shaped 20,000mAh bank would bulge the bag — for commuters and students, that is the difference between carrying a laptop charger and leaving it at home. The Nano in real use

The Nano is the bank you actually carry, because at 215g with a cable built in it disappears into a jacket pocket. The built-in USB-C cable is the whole idea: it charges your phone, recharges the bank itself, and doubles as a carrying handle, so the most common power-bank failure — a charged bank and no cable — cannot happen. The limit to understand is the fine print on Anker's own spec page: 30W is the single-port maximum, while total output across all ports at once is 24W — so the Nano fast-charges one device at a time, and charging a phone and earbuds together splits the power. For a phone and a pair of earbuds that is irrelevant; for a phone and a tablet at once, it is the reason to step up to the Blade or the Prime.

How to Choose the Right Power Bank

Start with what you charge. A laptop needs 100W: the Anker Prime delivers 200W total across dual 100W USB-C ports, so it can charge a laptop and a phone at once, while the Baseus Blade gives one 100W port among its four in a 0.7-inch flat profile that slips into a laptop sleeve. A phone alone needs only 30W, which is where the pocket-sized Anker Nano with its built-in cable wins — no cable to carry, no cable to forget. Next, check capacity against travel: the Prime (72Wh) and Blade (74Wh) are both marked TSA carry-on safe, while the Nano's 10,000mAh (~36Wh) is the lightest option for a day out.

GaN, PPS and PD 3.1: the jargon that decides your charge speed

Three acronyms decide whether a power bank actually fast-charges your device, and none of them is the milliamp number on the box. GaN (gallium nitride) is the transistor technology that lets a charger be smaller and cooler at high wattage — it is why a 200W bank fits in a pocket-sized brick instead of a laptop power brick. PPS (Programmable Power Supply) is the USB-PD feature Samsung phones need for full 45W Super Fast Charging 2.0; a bank without PPS tops a Galaxy flagship out at 25W no matter what its headline wattage says. And PD 3.1 is the standard behind outputs above 100W — this guide's banks top out at 200W total but 100W per port, so they use the older 100W ceiling and do not need PD 3.1's extended range. The practical check before buying is boring but decisive: match the bank's port wattage and PPS support to your phone or laptop, and match the cable too — a 100W port with a 60W cable is a 60W port.

Flying with a power bank: the 100Wh rule in practice

All three banks in this guide are legal in carry-on luggage, and the reason is a single number: 100 watt-hours. Aviation rules cap carry-on lithium batteries at 100Wh per battery without airline approval, and the Prime (72Wh), the Blade (74Wh) and the Nano (about 37Wh) all sit below it — which is why the table calls the first two TSA carry-on safe. Two habits keep it that way in practice. First, power banks go in carry-on, never in checked luggage, where they are restricted. Second, check the watt-hours printed on the bank itself rather than doing the milliamp math at the airport: 20,000mAh at the 3.7V cell voltage is 74Wh, but the same milliamp figure at a different voltage is a different number, and security staff read the label. One more 2026 wrinkle: a growing number of airlines now ban using power banks during the flight even while carrying them is allowed, so check your carrier's current rules before you board rather than assuming in-seat charging is fine.

What we'd buy and why

Buy the Anker Prime if you travel with two laptops, or a laptop and a tablet, and you want both at full speed: dual 100W ports are a capability nothing else in this guide offers, and the display removes the guesswork. Buy the Baseus Blade if you carry one laptop in a slim bag: the same 100W charging from one port in a flat 0.7-inch slab that fits a laptop sleeve, for roughly half the Prime's price. Buy the Anker Nano if you want a bank you will actually carry every day: 215g, a built-in cable, and 30W is everything a phone needs, and a bank left at home charges nothing. If you can only buy one and you own a laptop, buy the Blade — it is the middle of this guide in price and the most versatile shape. If you own only a phone, buy the Nano and spend the difference on a good 30W wall charger to refill it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Wh mean, and why does it matter for flights?

Watt-hours measure total energy: a 72Wh bank holds roughly twice the energy of a 36Wh one. Airlines cap carry-on batteries at 100Wh without approval, so both the 72Wh and 74Wh picks in this guide fly in your carry-on with no paperwork — checked luggage is where power banks are restricted.

100W or 30W — how much output do I need?

100W for laptops, 30W for most phones — but the newest iPhones peak higher: an iPhone 17 Pro needs a 40W+ output for Apple's fastest charge (~50% in 20 minutes), and the iPhone 18 Pro goes up to 60W. A 30W bank still charges any phone well; it just will not hit the newest Pros' top speed. A laptop charging at 30W crawls or drains under load, while a phone gains nothing from 100W it cannot use. Match the output to the hungriest device you will charge.

Can one bank charge a laptop and a phone together?

Yes, with the right bank: the Anker Prime's dual 100W USB-C ports are built for exactly that, splitting its 200W total across two devices. The Baseus Blade has four ports (2× USB-C + 2× USB-A), so it can charge a laptop and a phone together — though output splits to 65W + 30W when two devices share it. One USB-C port alone delivers the full 100W for a single laptop at full speed.

How many phone charges does 10,000mAh give?

Roughly two full charges for a typical phone, after conversion losses — the Nano's ~36Wh against a phone battery near 15Wh. For a weekend without a wall outlet, step up to the 20,000mAh class instead.

Is a built-in cable worth it?

For everyday carry, yes: the Nano's built-in cable means you never arrive with a charged bank and no way to connect it. For laptop charging, built-in cables are rare at 100W, so carry a proper USB-C cable with the bigger banks.
